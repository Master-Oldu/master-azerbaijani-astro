## 20 Yanvar

If you spend time with Azerbaijani, sooner or later you will come across **20 Yanvar**.

You may see it in a January news headline, hear it in a family conversation, or recognize it as the name of a metro station in Baku.

It means January 20. In conversations about Azerbaijan's history, those two words refer to one thing: the events of 1990, when Soviet troops entered Baku and killed civilians.

**Qara Yanvar** means *Black January*.

For many Azerbaijanis, it is a date connected to grief, family memory, and the struggle for independence. Understanding what happened helps you understand the language people use to remember it.


## What Happened

In 1990, Azerbaijan was still part of the Soviet Union. Demands for greater sovereignty were growing, driven by the conflict over Nagorno-Karabakh and mounting opposition to Soviet rule. Baku had also experienced deadly attacks against Armenian residents earlier that January.

Soviet troops entered the city on the night of January 19 to 20. The state of emergency was announced to the public only after the operation had already begun and people had already been killed.

According to [Azerbaijan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://mfa.gov.az/en/news/no00520nbspstatement-of-the-ministry-of-foreign-affairs-of-the-republic-of-azerbaijan-enru), the crackdown killed 147 civilians and seriously injured 744 people.

When you hear **20 Yanvar faciəsi**,  *the January 20 tragedy*, this is the event being remembered.

For background on how Soviet rule shaped Azerbaijan and its language more broadly, the [post on Soviet history and Azerbaijani](/blog/how-soviet-history-changed-azerbaijani-language) covers that period in more depth.

## The Language of Independence

Two words appear often in Azerbaijani commemorations of Qara Yanvar:

- **Azadlıq** - freedom
- **Müstəqillik** - independence

Those who died are honored in connection with Azerbaijan's struggle for both. That is why a speech about January 20 moves between grief and determination.

Azerbaijan adopted its Constitutional Act on State Independence on October 18, 1991. January 20 remains a day of mourning and an important part of how the road to independence is remembered.


## Ümumxalq Hüzn Günü

January 20 is observed as **Ümumxalq Hüzn Günü**, National Mourning Day.

**Hüzn** means sorrow or grief. **Ümumxalq** refers to the whole nation. Together they describe a shared, public act of mourning.

People visit **Şəhidlər xiyabanı**, the Alley of Martyrs in Baku, and lay flowers. At noon, a minute of silence is observed across the country.

**Bir dəqiqəlik sükut.** *A minute of silence.*

Across the country, ships sound their horns, traffic stops, and metro trains halt for that minute. That is what this day looks like in practice.
 
If you are in Azerbaijan on January 20, you will recognize what is happening around you.


## What Şəhid Means Here

**Şəhid** is usually translated as *martyr*. **Şəhidlər** is the plural.

In Azerbaijani, the word is used for someone honored for giving their life for their country or a cause. Around January 20, it refers to the civilians killed in the crackdown.

You will see **20 Yanvar şəhidləri**, *the martyrs of January 20*.

In this context, the word expresses national remembrance and deep respect. That is why it appears in speeches, memorial inscriptions, and in the name **Şəhidlər xiyabanı** itself.


## The Words Used to Remember

A few words will help you follow a commemorative message even without advanced Azerbaijani:

- **Xatirə** - memory, remembrance
- **Anmaq** - to remember, to commemorate
- **Yad etmək** - to remember, to pay tribute
- **Ehtiram** - deep respect
- **Faciə** - tragedy

Here is how they come together:

> **20 Yanvar şəhidlərinin xatirəsini ehtiramla anırıq.**  
> *We respectfully commemorate the memory of the January 20 martyrs.*

**Xatirəsini anırıq** tells you someone's memory is being honored. **Ehtiramla** adds *with respect*. You may also hear **yad edirik** in place of **anırıq**; both are used in the language of remembrance.

Once you recognize these expressions, a headline or memorial message becomes readable, even when you do not know every word. The formal register these words belong to, the use of **siz**, of **ehtiram**, of careful phrasing, is part of a broader pattern in how Azerbaijani handles respect, covered in the [post on formality and register](/blog/understanding-respect-and-formality-in-azerbaijani-culture).

## When Someone Shares Their Memories

For someone who lived through January 1990, the date may bring back where they were, what they heard, and who they lost. A younger person may know it through a parent or grandparent's account.

If someone is comfortable talking about it, you can ask:

> **O günləri necə xatırlayırsınız?**  
> *How do you remember those days?*

Or:

> **Bu gün sizin üçün nə deməkdir?**  
> *What does this day mean to you?*

Both use the respectful **siz** form. Let the person choose how much they want to share.

For heritage learners, a conversation like this can connect a familiar date to family history. You may have heard **20 Yanvar** for years without knowing the full story. It is okay to begin by listening.


## Language Carries Memory

Knowing about Qara Yanvar changes what you hear in **20 Yanvar**, **şəhidlər**, and **xatirə**.

You understand why a date appears in a station name. Why people gather with flowers. Why certain words are spoken with care.

As your Azerbaijani grows, you can read accounts of that time and listen to people tell their own stories. The history gives you context. The language lets you hear those stories in the words people actually choose.
