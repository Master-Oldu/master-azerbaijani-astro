**You Know This Word. OR Do You?**

Imagine someone tells you:

> **Sabah toy var.**

You recognize *toy*. Easy enough. Something for children to play with, right?

Not quite.

In Azerbaijani, **toy** means wedding. Someone is telling you there is a wedding tomorrow, not announcing the arrival of a new Lego set.

Welcome to the world of false friends.

False friends are words in two languages that look or sound similar but mean different things. Sometimes the resemblance is pure coincidence. Sometimes the words have related histories but developed different meanings.

Either way, they create a particular problem for language learners: the word looks familiar, so your brain decides it already knows the answer.

And that is how you end up misunderstanding a perfectly ordinary conversation.


## Same Spelling, Completely Different Meaning

These are the entertaining ones. They look exactly like English words, but the Azerbaijani meaning takes you somewhere else entirely.

### Toy

In English, a toy is something a child plays with. In Azerbaijani, **toy** is a wedding.

Azerbaijani weddings are a major part of family and social life, so this is a word you will encounter often, especially if you have Azerbaijani relatives or friends.

> **Sabah toy var.** - There is a wedding tomorrow.  
> **Toy nə vaxtdır?** - When is the wedding?

If someone invites you to a *toy*, expect music, food, dancing, and plenty of guests. You are not going to a toy store.

### Boy

In English, a boy is a male child. In Azerbaijani, **boy** means height, length, or stature.

> **Boyun neçədir?** - How tall are you?  
> **Onun boyu uzundur.** - He or she is tall.

The Azerbaijani word for a boy is **oğlan**. So when someone mentions *boy*, pay attention to the surrounding sentence; they are likely talking about someone's height, not someone's son.

### Son

In English, son is a male child in relation to his parents. In Azerbaijani, **son** means end, last, or final.

> **Bu son dəfədir.** - This is the last time.  
> **Filmin sonu maraqlıdır.** - The ending of the movie is interesting.

The Azerbaijani word for son in the family sense is **oğul**. Context decides which language's meaning applies, and in Azerbaijani, *son* almost always signals the end of something.

### Can

In English, *can* expresses ability or possibility. In Azerbaijani, **can** means life, soul, and sits at the center of some of the warmest expressions in the language.

> **Canım, necəsən?** - My dear, how are you?  
> **Sən mənim canımsan.** - You are my life.

Notice **canım**, an affectionate way of addressing someone close to you. This word carries more weight than a dictionary entry can show. It shows up in endearments, in expressions of deep feeling.

One more thing worth knowing: Azerbaijani **c** sounds like the English **j** in *jam*, so *can* is not pronounced anything like the English word.


## The Short Words That Catch You Off Guard

Some false friends are only two or three letters long. English speakers recognize them instantly, which is exactly the problem.

### It

In English, *it* is a pronoun. In Azerbaijani, **it** means dog.

> **Həyətdə it var.** - There is a dog in the yard.  
> **Bu mənim itimdir.** - This is my dog.

Straightforward enough. Just remember that Azerbaijani *it* refers to an animal, not a grammatical stand-in. And yes, it can function as an insult in Azerbaijani depending on context, not a word to throw around carelessly.

### At

In English, *at* is a preposition. In Azerbaijani, **at** means horse.

> **At qaçır.** - The horse is running.  
> **Mən atları sevirəm.** - I love horses.

An ordinary, everyday Azerbaijani word that happens to look exactly like one of the most frequently used prepositions in English.

### On

In English, *on* is a preposition. In Azerbaijani, **on** means ten.

> **On dəqiqə gözlə.** - Wait ten minutes.  
> **On manat.** - Ten manats.

Numbers come up everywhere, shopping, time, prices, and addresses. If you see *on* next to a unit of time or money, the English interpretation will lead you astray.

### Dad

In English, *dad* is an informal word for father. In Azerbaijani, **dad** means taste or flavor.

> **Bu yeməyin dadı yaxşıdır.** - This food tastes good.  
> **Dadına bax.** - Taste it.

If an Azerbaijani family member tells you *dadına bax*, they are asking you to try the food. They are not asking you to locate anyone's father.

### Top

In English, *top* is the uppermost part of something. In Azerbaijani, **top** means ball, the kind used in sports and play.

> **Top haradadır?** - Where is the ball?  
> **Uşaqlar top oynayır.** - The children are playing ball.

*Top* also carries the meaning of cannon in Azerbaijani, but when children are playing outside, it is almost certainly a ball.


## The Borrowed Words That Look Trustworthy

Not every false friend is an exact spelling match. Some are more deceptive because they resemble familiar international vocabulary. You might assume they were borrowed with the same meaning.

Sometimes they were not.

### Mağaza

It looks close to English *magazine*, but **mağaza** means a shop or store.

> **Mağazaya gedirəm.** - I am going to the store.

This one actually has a shared history. Both words ultimately trace back to an Arabic word meaning storehouse. English *magazine* later developed its familiar meaning of a publication, while Azerbaijani **mağaza** means a shop or store. Same roots, different destinations.

If you want to say *magazine* as in a publication, the Azerbaijani word is **jurnal**.

### Aktual

In English, *actual* means real or existing. Azerbaijani **aktual** means current, timely, or relevant to the present situation.

> **Bu mövzu aktualdır.** - This topic is relevant right now.

You will see this word in discussions, news, and professional contexts. For the English meaning of *actual*, Azerbaijani words like **həqiqi** fit better depending on the sentence.

### Prospekt

In English, a *prospect* is a future possibility or opportunity. In Azerbaijani, **prospekt** refers to a broad avenue or major city street.

> **Azadlıq prospekti** - you will see this on street signs in Baku.

It is a place, not a career outlook. The street-name usage is the one you will encounter most.


## The Context Is Your Best Defense

Toy, boy, son, it, at, on, dad, top. Every one of those is ordinary English vocabulary. Each one means something completely different in Azerbaijani.

Seeing a word in a full sentence gives you clues that an isolated vocabulary list cannot. The surrounding words, the situation, and the grammar all help you understand what it means.

Take **On dəqiqə gözlə**. The word *dəqiqə* means minute. Once you recognize it, *on* as a number makes immediate sense. Or **Toy nə vaxtdır?**; the question is asking when something will take place. If you know the Azerbaijani meaning of *toy*, the sentence falls into place.

A word on its own gives you a definition. A word in a sentence gives you a reason to remember it.

Azerbaijani words also change form through suffixes; you may learn *toy* and later encounter *toya* or *toyda*, you may learn *boy* and encounter *boyu*. The [suffixes post](/blog/azerbaijani-suffixes-the-building-blocks-of-fluency) covers how that system works if you want to go deeper.


## A Quick Meaning Check

Before moving on, see what your English instincts tell you about these sentences. These use the same vocabulary but in different forms.

1. **Sabah toya gedirik.**
2. **On gün sonra görüşərik.**
3. **Dadına bax, çox dadlıdır.**
4. **Bu mağaza bağlıdır.**
5. **Uşaqlar həyətdə top oynayırlar.**

The answers:

1. We are going to a wedding tomorrow.
2. We will see each other in ten days.
3. Taste it, it is very tasty.
4. This store is closed.
5. The children are playing ball in the yard.

If your first interpretation was different, you have just experienced exactly why false friends are worth paying attention to.


## Familiar Does Not Mean Correct

One of the advantages of learning a language that uses a familiar alphabet is that you can start recognizing words quickly.

The disadvantage is that sometimes you recognize the wrong thing.

You see a word that looks English, assign it an English meaning, and move on before checking whether the sentence makes sense. False friends expose that habit.

The solution is not to become suspicious of every familiar-looking word. Plenty of words really do share meanings across languages. It is to give the Azerbaijani sentence a chance to tell you what the word means before your English instincts make the decision.

After all, being invited to a *toy* is much more exciting when you know there is a wedding waiting for you.
