## What You Will Not Find in a Textbook

Textbooks teach you how to introduce yourself, ask for directions, and conjugate verbs.

These things are rarely covered in beginner lessons: how to call someone out for pretending not to know something, what your friend means when they ask if you have eaten a heart, or the expression someone uses when they have not seen you in months and want to know where on earth you disappeared to.

That is what this post is for.

If you want the background on how spoken Azerbaijani differs from the written standard, the [standard vs spoken post](/blog/standard-vs-spoken-azerbaijani-why-what-you-learn-doesnt-match-what-you-hear) covers that. And if you want to understand particles, pronoun dropping, and compressions, the [natural speech post](/blog/how-to-sound-natural-in-azerbaijani-breaking-out-of-textbook-speech) goes into those. Here we are looking at vocabulary, expressions, and the kind of language people reach for in casual conversation.

Learn these with context. The relationship and the situation matter as much as the words.


## Qaqa, Nə Var, Nə Yox?

You hear two friends greet each other:

> **Qaqa, nə var, nə yox?** - Bro, how's it going?

You may already know **qardaş** means brother. **Qaqa** and **qaqaş** are the familiar, casual versions you hear between male friends. The person being addressed is usually a friend, not a brother. The word is about the tone of the relationship, not the literal family connection.

**Nə var, nə yox** is the everyday "how's it going" or "what's new." Simple, casual, and something you will hear constantly.

A natural reply is **elə-belə** or **belə də**, which covers both "so-so" when someone asks how you are, and "nothing much" when someone asks what you are doing. Context separates the two.


## Harda İtib Batmısan?

Said to a friend you have not seen in a long time:

> **Harda itib batmısan?** - Where have you disappeared to?

Literally, it asks where someone has been lost. Between close friends, it can be a warm, teasing way to ask where someone has been. You say it to someone close. A stranger would find it odd.


## Özünü Öldürsə, 15 Manatdan Yuxarı Deyil

One of the most dramatic ways to express disbelief about a price:

> **Özünü öldürsə, 15 manatdan yuxarı deyil.** - It is worth 15 manat at the absolute most.

Literally: even if it kills itself, it is not worth more than 15 manat.

Nobody is killing anything. This is just how strongly the speaker believes the price should not be higher than 15 manat. The exaggeration is the point. You will hear this kind of construction whenever someone wants to make clear they find something overpriced or overvalued.


## Ürək Yemisən?

Said when someone suggests something daring, risky, or bold:

> **Ürək yemisən?** - Have you eaten a heart?

In English, you might say "are you brave enough" or "do you have the guts." In Azerbaijani, the image is eating a heart. The question is not literal. It is a way of asking whether someone has the courage for whatever is being proposed.


## Əlim Ağzımda Qaldı

When something leaves you completely stunned:

> **Əlim ağzımda qaldı.** - I was stunned. / I couldn't believe it.

Literally: my hand was left on my mouth. Picture someone so shocked that they raised their hand to their mouth, and it stayed there. The expression broadly means astonishment. It often comes up when someone does something you would never have expected from them, and usually not in a pleasant way.


## Əlimdə Böyüyüb

Said warmly about someone you watched grow up:

> **Əlimdə böyüyüb.** - He/she grew up in my hands.

This is affectionate and proud. You use it when you were closely involved in someone's upbringing or simply present throughout their life. A parent, an older relative, a family friend might say this. The feeling is one of affection and shared history.


## Beş Qəpiklik Adam Deyil

Said behind someone's back, about their character:

> **Beş qəpiklik adam deyil.** - He/she is not worth five pennies.

This is a judgment on someone's moral worth, not their usefulness or competence. It is what you say when you think a person is bad news, untrustworthy, or someone you want nothing to do with. You say it behind their back. Unless things have escalated into an open confrontation, and then it might come out face-to-face.


## Gəl Gir Ağzımın İçinə

This one is rude. Worth knowing because you may hear it, but not something to reach for casually:

> **Gəl gir ağzımın içinə.** - Come get inside my mouth.

Someone says this when they feel crowded or pushed past what they can tolerate. It is sharp, it signals real irritation, and it is not something that comes out in polite company.


## Əşi

A short word that carries a lot depending on how it lands:

> **Əşi, tez ol!** - Come on, hurry up!  
> **Əşi, boş ver.** - Oh, let it go.

Think of it as "oh come on" or "oh please." It can carry mild impatience, dismissal, or exasperation. Said lightly, it brushes something aside. Said sharply, it signals real irritation. The word itself gives you a direction; the tone tells you how far the speaker has gone.


## Başını Burax

The Azerbaijani way to say "never mind" or "let it go":

> **Başını burax, gedək.** - Let it go, let's move on.

Literally, it means "let the head go." Depending on context, it can mean letting a matter drop or leaving a person alone. **Başını burax** said about a situation means forget it. Said about a person, it means leave them be. You may also hear **boş ver** used in the same way. Both are used in casual speech.


## Zad

A loose, casual word for "thing," "stuff," or "or something":

> **Bu zad nədir?** - What is this thing?  
> **Kitab-zad aldın?** - Did you buy any books or anything?

When someone adds **zad** to a word or uses it on its own, they are speaking loosely. Sometimes the situation already gives enough context and precision is not needed. Sometimes the exact word just will not come to mind. Either way, you will hear it constantly in relaxed conversation.


## Guya

The skepticism word:

> **Guya bilmirsən!** - As if you don't know!

When someone is pretending not to know something, or when a claim seems hard to believe, **guya** signals that the speaker is not buying it. Between friends it can be teasing. In an argument, it sharpens quickly. It is worth recognizing early and using carefully, because the tone carries most of the meaning.


## Ay Sağ Ol

You already know **sağ ol** as thank you. **Ay sağ ol** goes further. It adds warmth and approval, and in the right moment, it works like "exactly" or "that's it":

> **Yəni sabah görüşürük?** - So we are meeting tomorrow?  
> **Ay sağ ol, elə onu deyirəm.** - Exactly, that is what I have been saying.

You will also hear it when someone finally understands something after you have been trying to explain it, or when someone does something thoughtful. The feeling is a mix of gratitude and relief that the person got it right.


## Start by Listening

Most of these you will not produce on day one. But you will hear them, and understanding what is happening in the conversation matters more than being able to say it yourself right away.

Start by recognizing them. Notice who says them, to whom, and what prompted it. That is how you build a sense of when they belong in your own speech.
