**It Looks Familiar OR It's Not.**

The Azerbaijani alphabet uses Latin letters. At first glance, it looks like something you could just read.

Then you try to say **Xəzər** or **Qarabağ** out loud and realize your mouth has no idea what it is doing.

The problem isn't the alphabet. The problem is that some letters look exactly like English letters but do not sound like them at all. Others look strange but are easier than they appear. And the vowels follow their own logic entirely.

In [Azerbaijani Alphabet Made Easy](/blog/azerbaijani-alphabet-made-easy), we covered the full alphabet. Here, we are focusing on the letters that cause the most trouble, why English speakers misread them, and what it takes to pronounce them correctly.

The categories below, **True Latin Twins, False Friends, and Oddballs**, are ones I created to help learners approach the alphabet without feeling overwhelmed. You will not find this framework in a textbook. It is how I organized the first lesson of the Master Azerbaijani app, and it is what I have found works best for English speakers starting out.


## The True Latin Twins

These letters look familiar and sound close enough to their English equivalents that your instincts mostly serve you: **b, d, f, h, k, l, m, n, p, s, t, y, z**.

One worth noting: **r**. Azerbaijani r is a tapped or lightly trilled sound, produced with the tongue tip touching just behind the upper teeth. It is not the soft, pulled-back English r. Think of the r in Spanish or Italian rather than the r in "rain." It is not difficult to approximate, but it is different, and English speakers who do not notice this will carry their English r into Azerbaijani without realizing it.


## The False Friends

These letters look familiar but don't sound the way an English speaker expects. This is where most pronunciation mistakes happen.

### C

In English, C makes either an "s" sound or a "k" sound depending on the word. In Azerbaijani, **c** is always the "j" sound in **Jane**. Always. No exceptions.

> **can** - soul (sounds like "jan")  
> **cümlə** - sentence

If you read **can** as the English word "can," you are already in the wrong place.

### Q and G

These two are worth explaining together because the distinction between them matters.

Azerbaijani **q** is approximately the hard G in **go**. That is your starting point. Azerbaijani **g** is produced further forward in the mouth, with a palatal quality that makes it sit closer to the front than Q does.

The two letters make related but distinct sounds, and the distinction matters. Q is the deeper one; G is the more forward one. Collapsing them into the same English G loses that difference.

> **qız**-— girl  
> **qara** - black  
> **gəl** - come  
> **gün** - day

When you say these words, Q should feel deeper and further back than G. That gap is what you are training.

### X

Forget English X entirely. Azerbaijani **x** is a voiceless fricative produced at the back of the throat, like the "ch" in Scottish **loch** or the "ch" in German **Bach**. A continuous, raspy sound, not a stop.

> **xoş** - pleasant  
> **Xəzər** - Caspian  
> **xalq** - people

To practice: say "loch" slowly and hold the final sound. Now try saying **xoş** starting from that place in your throat.

English speakers either say "ks" or drop the sound entirely. Neither is close enough.

### J

In English, J sounds like "dj" in **jump**. Azerbaijani **j** is the soft "zh" sound, like the J in the French word **je** or the S in the English word **measure**.

> **jurnal** - journal  
> **janr** - genre

A hard English "dj" will be understood, but it is not correct.


## The Oddballs

These letters do not exist as standalone letters in English. The sounds themselves may not be completely foreign, but the letter form is, and that gap causes its own problems.

### Ç

This is the "ch" sound in **chair**. Not difficult once you know it, but the letter itself creates hesitation because **ç** does not exist in English.

> **çay** - tea  
> **çox** - very, a lot  
> **çörək** - bread

### Ş

The "sh" sound in **shape**. Same situation: the sound exists in English, the standalone letter does not, and breaking the habit takes deliberate repetition.

My own name is **Şəhla**. Even after years of writing it in English as Shahla, my hand still instinctively reaches for Ş instead of Sh. To me, that has always been one letter, not two. It is a small habit, but it shows how deeply the alphabet you grow up with shapes the way you read and write.

> **şəhər** - city  
> **şirin** - sweet  
> **Şəhla** - a name you might now recognize

### Ğ

This one is genuinely new for most English speakers. Speakers of some Arabic varieties or certain other languages may recognize a similar throat articulation, but that does not guarantee immediate accuracy. It takes deliberate practice regardless.

Azerbaijani **ğ** is a voiced fricative, the voiced counterpart of X. Unlike the Turkish ğ, which is often weakened or barely present, Azerbaijani ğ is a real sound. Don't treat it as silent.

The airflow doesn't stop the way it does with Q. The sound is continuous, produced from the same general area as X but with your vocal cords engaged.

> **dağ** - mountain  
> **sağ** - right, healthy  
> **bağ** - garden

To practice: produce the X sound from Scottish **loch**. Now let your vocal cords vibrate while keeping everything else the same. That voiced version is ğ, much like French r.


## The Vowels

Azerbaijani has nine vowels. Some are familiar. Some have no direct English equivalent. These are the ones that take the most deliberate practice.

**A, E, O, U** are comparatively familiar. English vowel letters vary a great deal depending on the word and accent, but these vowels are more consistent in Azerbaijani, which actually makes them easier to learn once you hear them in context.

**İ** (dotted i) is the "ee" sound in **meet**. Note that the capital version carries the dot: **İ**. This matters because the dotless **I** is a different letter with a completely different sound.

### Ə

A near-open front unrounded vowel, similar to the "a" in American English **fan**. Not the "e" in "bed" and not the long "a" in "face." The mouth is more open than either.

> **əl** - hand  
> **gəl** - come  
> **dərs** - lesson

English speakers most often substitute a plain short "e." It is close but not accurate.

### I (dotless i)

No direct English equivalent. This is the vowel English speakers struggle with most. It is produced at the back of the mouth without rounding the lips, somewhere between the short "u" in **upon** and the "i" in **bit**, but without the sharpness of either.

To practice: say "oo" as in **food**. Keep your tongue in that position, then unround your lips without moving your tongue. The result should be somewhere near the Azerbaijani ı.

> **qız** - girl  
> **bıçaq** - knife  

### Ö

Round your lips as if you are about to say "o." Keep them rounded, but move your tongue forward as if you were going to say "e." Hold that position and let sound come out. That is the Azerbaijani ö, the same sound as German **ö** or French **eu**.

> **göz** - eye  
> **gözəl** - beautiful

### Ü

Round your lips as if you are about to say "oo." Keep them rounded, but move your tongue forward and up as if you were going to say "ee." That is ü, the same sound as German **ü** or French **u** in "lune."

> **gün** - day  
> **üz** - face  
> **üzüm** - grape


## Practice Sentences

Once you know which sounds to work on, try these sentences. They bring several of the tricky letters together in real words.

> **Xoş gəldiniz.** - Welcome. (x, ə, g)

> **Qız gözəldir.** - The girl is beautiful. (q, ı, g, ö, ə)

> **Dağ çox böyükdür.** - The mountain is very big. (ğ, ç, ö, ü)

Say each one slowly, then faster. Notice which sound makes you pause. That is the one to spend more time on.


## The Real Problem

The next time you struggle to pronounce an Azerbaijani word, pay attention to which sound is causing the problem. Is it genuinely unfamiliar, or are you reading it the way English taught you?

Those are two different problems and they need two different fixes.

You already know how to make more Azerbaijani sounds than you might think. Sometimes the hardest part is convincing your English instincts to stay out of the way.

The method that works best for getting these sounds into your mouth is shadowing; listening to a native speaker and repeating what you hear, matching the sounds and rhythm as closely as you can. The [shadowing post](/blog/the-power-of-shadowing-speak-like-a-native) covers exactly how to do that.
